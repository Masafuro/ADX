![ADX Reference CARD v0](reference_card_v0_20261109.png "Fig 1: ADX Reference CARD v0 — an 88.0 mm × 54.0 mm reference board with every mounting keep-out, clearance trace, and bus pinout etched directly onto the PCB silkscreen.")

In CAD, moving a boundary line by one millimeter is trivial. You grab a vector, type `+1.0mm`, hit Enter, and admire the symmetry on your monitor.

On the physical assembly floor, however, that single millimeter dictates whether a board flows smoothly through production or generates continuous assembly friction.

Over the past few weeks, our team has been wrestling with an uncomfortable reality check. For months, the ADX project was anchored around our standardized **8748 Form Factor** (87.0 mm × 48.0 mm). It was clean, compact, and designed around a classic right-angle IDC ejector header.

Yet, as we moved closer to small-batch panel fabrication and real 3D-printed enclosure trials, friction points began surfacing. Corner chamfers that looked sleek on screen made panel manufacturing cumbersome. Mounting holes that were nominally M3-compatible left almost zero structural meat under real washer clamping force. And sliding boards into slot-in enclosures kept catching on protruding through-hole solder pins.

None of these issues were fatal on a single prototype hand-soldered on a workbench. But the moment you plan for batches of 50 to 100 units, they compound into noticeable assembly bottlenecks and potential field failures.

To solve this, we stepped away from pure circuit schematics and looked to one of the central tenets of the Toyota Production System (TPS):

> **"後工程はお客様" (Ato-kōtei wa Okyaku-sama) — *The Next Process Is Your Customer.***

In manufacturing, the person or machine at the next workstation is not just downstream; they are your customer. If you pass along a part that requires extra jiggling, filing, deburring, or guesswork, you have introduced friction into the system.

Here is how applying that mindset prompted a careful, millimeter-by-millimeter review of our board profile—and why we are currently testing an experimental evolution: the **8854 Form Factor** (88.0 mm × 54.0 mm).

---

## 1. Customer #1: The V-Cut Saw (Why We Stepped Away from C3 Chamfers)

When we originally drafted the 8748 standard, we specified a **C3 (3 mm × 45°) chamfer** on all four corners. It gave the bare board a finished, industrial feel and looked great in 3D CAD renders.

Then we looked closely at how panelized PCBs are actually separated at modern turnkey fabs like JLCPCB.

```text
[ The C3 Chamfer Dilemma ]
     ▲
     │  Continuous V-Cut Blade Pass (Blocked!)
─────┼─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─
     │ \  <── Chamfer prevents straight scoring!
     │  │
     │  │  PCB Body
```

To slice an array of boards quickly and cleanly without wasting FR4, board fabs rely on **V-scoring (V-cut)**: high-speed rotary blades that score straight, continuous grooves across an entire panel from edge to edge.

The moment you introduce corner chamfers, **a continuous straight V-cut is no longer an option**. The fab is forced to switch to CNC router milling with perforated breakaway tabs ("mouse bites"):
- You lose usable panel space because router bits need 2 mm of milling clearance between adjacent boards.
- Breaking tabs leaves small fiberglass burrs that require manual deburring before boards slide cleanly into tight enclosures.
- Milling machine time adds subtle turnaround and PCBA unit cost penalties.

By treating the panel fab and de-paneling operator as our customers, the design conclusion was straightforward: **drop the corner chamfers in favor of straight, unchamfered edges**.

On the 8854 prototype, we returned to clean 90° corners (C0). The result is continuous, full-pass V-scoring across the entire panel. No manual deburring, no mouse-bite tabs, and clean, uniform edges straight from the factory.

## 2. Customer #2: The Human QA Inspector (Miller's Law at 2×3)

Once panels are populated via SMT, someone has to inspect component placement, verify solder joints, and probe testpoints.

When calculating panel economics, the default engineering impulse is to pack as many boards into a panel as physically possible. If 2 × 3 = 6 pcs fits, why not squeeze 2 × 4 = 8 pcs?

Here is where cognitive ergonomics intersects with PCB panelization. In 1956, cognitive psychologist George A. Miller published his classic paper on working memory limits: ***The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two*** (7 ± 2).

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│          【 Panel Matrix & Cognitive Batching 】           │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2 × 4 (8 pcs): Exceeds Miller's Law. Rapid visual fatigue. │
│                Panel width exceeds standard price tier.    │
│                                                            │
│ 2 × 3 (6 pcs): Under magical number 7! Instant subitizing. │
│                186 mm × 162 mm: Exact fab price optimum.   │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

When an inspector looks at an array of **6 units**, the human visual system can count and verify them almost instantly without conscious serial counting—a process known as *subitizing*. Once you push to 8 units per panel, visual fatigue sets in noticeably faster, and the likelihood of overlooking a cold joint or reversed diode polarity increases.

Furthermore, the mechanical math aligns cleanly:
- Two columns of 88 mm plus 5 mm tooling rails on each side = **186 mm**.
- Three rows of 54 mm = **162 mm**.
- Total panel size: **186 mm × 162 mm**.

This lands directly inside the optimal manufacturing and pricing tier (150 ~ 200 mm) of standard fab services. Respecting the inspector's cognitive bandwidth ended up lowering our assembly risk while staying within the fab's economic sweet spot.

## 3. Customer #3: The Field Tech with a Screwdriver (The 1 mm Offset)

If you have ever tightened an M3 screw into an enclosure standoff and felt that uneasy sensation of a steel washer biting too close to a board edge, you understand why mounting hole margins matter.

In our original 8748 specification, mounting hole centers were placed 5.0 mm from the board edges, with a Φ10.0 mm (R = 5.0 mm) washer keep-out.

```text
Old 8748: Hole center at 5.0 mm         New 8854: Hole center at 6.0 mm
┌──────────────                         ┌──────────────
│    5.0 mm                             │    6.0 mm
│  ├───────► (O) Hole                   │  ├─────────► (O) Hole
│ ┌───────────┐                         │   ┌───────────┐
│ │  Φ10 Keep-│                         │   │  Φ10 Keep-│
│ │   out     │                         │   │   out     │
│ └───────────┘                         │   └───────────┘
│ ◄─ 0 mm margin! (Tangent to edge)     │ ◄─ 1.0 mm solid FR4 meat! ──►│
```

At 5.0 mm, the edge of a standard Φ10.0 mm industrial spring washer sits precisely tangent to the edge of the board. The margin of solid FR4 fiberglass between the outer washer perimeter and empty air is **effectively zero**. Any minor routing variation or slight screw tilt during field installation risks chipping or cracking the PCB corner.

On the 8854 design, we shifted the mounting holes inward by **exactly 1.0 mm**—from 5.0 mm to **6.0 mm** from the edges:
- X Pitch: 88 - (6 × 2) = **76.0 mm** (was 77.0 mm)
- Y Pitch: 54 - (6 × 2) = **42.0 mm** (was 38.0 mm)

That single millimeter ensures that even with a heavy Φ10 mm washer or wide nylon standoff fully torqued down, there is still **a solid 1.0 mm buffer of structural substrate** protecting the corner from fracturing. The field technician can fasten an enclosure screw with an electric driver with genuine mechanical confidence.

## 4. Customer #4: The Enclosure Assembler (SMD-Only Underside)

Back in August, we explored adding physical U-shaped notches into the sides of the board (`hardware/Formfactor/memo.md`) so 3D-printed enclosure latches could snap into place. However, milled notches complicate edge routing, create stress concentration points, and interrupt clean V-cut lines.

Instead of cutting into the board outline, we shifted our focus to **copper and component placement rules on the underside.**

```text
  [ Front I/O Edge: 66.0 mm (Terminal Blocks / Connectors) ]
 ┌───────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┬───────────┐
 │  SMD ONLY │                                          │  SMD ONLY │
 │  UNDERSIDE│          Through-Hole Component Area     │  UNDERSIDE│
 │ (No leads)│                                          │ (No leads)│
 └───────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┴───────────┘
   ▲ 11.0 mm                                              ▲ 11.0 mm
   │                                                      │
   └────── Smooth glide zones for enclosure slide-rails ──┘
```

On 8854, we defined a **66.0 mm central I/O Edge** along the front. Outside of that 66 mm span (11.0 mm on each side), the entire underside layer is designated **SMD Only**.

Through-hole component leads (which leave sharp, clipped pin ends after wave or selective soldering) are strictly prohibited in these wings. When the board slides into an extruded aluminum channel or clicks into 3D-printed snap-fit tabs, the case rails glide over flat soldermask without snagging on sharp pins or scraping protective coatings.

## 5. The Reference Card: Turning the PCB into Its Own Datasheet

Few things disrupt prototyping momentum like digging through multi-page PDF mechanical drawings just to verify a mounting hole pitch or connector offset.

So we designed **ADX Reference CARD v0**.

Instead of leaving the prototype blank, we placed the key dimensions and layout rules directly onto the silkscreen:
- Origin coordinates in both metric and mils (`(0|0)` lower-left).
- Center point markings `(44|27)`.
- Board outline trace clearances (`20 mil`).
- Card guide rail keep-outs (`3 mm`).
- The 20-Pin vertical IDC bus pinout and 5V power sourcing rules (4.75V ~ 5.25V, ~2A OCP, reverse-current protection).

When held in your hand, the board serves as a physical, tactile calibration tool. You can place an M3 screw directly onto the silkscreen circles, verify connector alignment, or test-fit it into an enclosure to evaluate clearances in real space.

---

## 6. An Open Experiment, Not a Fixed Dogma

We want to emphasize: **we have not frozen 8854 as a permanent, immutable standard yet.**

Hardware design is a continuous dialogue with manufacturing and mechanical constraints. Moving from 8748 to 8854 adds 6 mm to the board depth (48 mm → 54 mm). Although shifting from horizontal right-angle IDC headers to vertical IDC headers noticeably shrunk the *overall* enclosure depth (no protruding cable bend radius at the rear), a slightly wider PCB does consume marginally more raw FR4.

We are currently running test panels with JLCPCB and printing iterative test enclosures to evaluate how these dimensions perform under actual assembly and handling conditions.

> **Key Takeaway:**  
> When designing a PCB, consider more than just the schematic netlist. Look downstream. Keep in mind the V-cut blade, the visual limits of the inspector, the enclosure slide rail, and the field tech's screwdriver. When every downstream process is treated as your customer, robust, reliable hardware follows naturally.
