In modern prototyping, "USB One-Cable Debug"—plugging in a single Type-C cable to supply power, flash firmware, and view serial telemetry—is taken for granted. Whether you are using a Raspberry Pi Pico or an ESP32 devkit, it is the undisputed default on every maker's workbench.

For the past several months, we tried repeatedly to bring that same convenience to our industrial core controller (the long-dormant CORE-U project).

We failed, repeatedly.

It took dozens of design iterations, rejected prototypes, and a painful economic reality check to realize something uncomfortable: **single-cable USB debugging is an anti-pattern for field-deployed industrial controllers.**

Here is the story of how 100-piece production limits, Arduino's vintage barrel jack, and a $0.15 RISC-V chip forced us to rethink hardware ergonomics from scratch.

---

## 1. The Small-Batch Wall: Why ATtiny1616 Hit a Ceiling

When you design open hardware, you are competing against the staggering economies of scale enjoyed by giants like the Raspberry Pi Foundation or Arduino.

A Raspberry Pi Pico sells for $4 to $5. Arduino clones sell for $3. Behind those prices are dedicated high-speed SMT lines running 24/7 and wafer-level component procurement across hundreds of thousands of units.

When you are an indie hardware developer manufacturing in **100-piece runs (100 pcs / 1 lot)** using turn-key PCBA services like JLCPCB or PCBWay, the economics look completely different:
- Base SMT setup fees and stencil costs weigh heavily on unit pricing.
- Each unique BOM line and Extended Component (requiring manual feeder changes) adds immediate fee penalties.
- Components are bought on cut-tape rather than full reels, eliminating volume discounts.

Our initial architecture was centered around Microchip’s 8-bit **ATtiny1616** (the heart of our CORE-A5). It is virtually indestructible, noise-immune, and boots instantaneously. But adding USB to it was a trap.

Because the ATtiny lacks an integrated USB PHY, building a USB-capable board required a 2-chip design: an external USB-to-UART bridge IC (such as a CH340 or FTDI), an external crystal oscillator, and a cluster of passive pass-through components.

```text
[ ATtiny1616 USB Concept (Failed) ]
  USB Connector ──> [ USB Bridge IC ] ──> [ External Crystal ]
                           │
                           ▼ (UART)
                   [ ATtiny1616 MCU ] (Flash: 16KB, SRAM: 2KB)
  Total PCBA Cost in 100-pc Run: ~$12-$15+  (For a 16KB 8-bit MCU!)
```

In a 100-piece run, the component count and assembly fees alone ballooned the board’s bare manufacturing cost to nearly three times the retail price of a Raspberry Pi Pico—all for a chip with **16 KB of Flash and 2 KB of SRAM**. Trying to squeeze a robust USB stack and application logic into that footprint left almost zero room for the user.

### The Temptation of "Feature Creep"
To justify that cost, we caught ourselves falling into classic feature-creep traps.
- At one point, we considered adding a through-hole (THT) prototyping breadboard area directly onto the empty PCB space of the 8748 board.
- But soldering sensors directly onto the CPU board completely destroyed our primary architectural rule: **instant, screw-free board replacement in the field**. Prototyping belongs on dedicated, front-edge expansion Cards, not on the Core controller.

We had to strip away the clutter and find a microcontroller with native silicon support.

## 2. From CH32V003 (CX3) to CH32X035 (CORE-CX5)

When WCH released the famous **CH32V003** (a $0.10 RISC-V chip), we briefly investigated a concept codenamed "CORE-CX3." But the V003 still lacked a USB PHY and topped out at 16 KB Flash, leaving us back at square one.

The true breakthrough came with WCH's **CH32X035G8U6** (QFN-28):
- **Integrated USB 2.0 Full-Speed PHY**: Zero external bridge IC, zero external crystals. Pure single-chip USB.
- **Spec Leap**: 32-bit RISC-V4C running at 48 MHz, **62 KB Flash**, and **20 KB SRAM** (a 4x Flash and 10x RAM upgrade over ATtiny1616).
- **Native 3.3V Logic with 5V Tolerance**: Drop-in compatible with our ADX bus standard.

By matching the silicon directly to our bus requirements, we eliminated an entire IC and half a dozen passives, bringing small-batch manufacturing costs back to reality. With the "035" silicon firmly locked in, the project graduated from the CX3 code-name to its official identity: **`ADX CORE-CX5`**.

## 3. The Mystery of Arduino's Barrel Jack

With native USB hardware in hand, we faced the next dilemma: **how should power be delivered?**

The obvious, modern answer was single-cable USB-C: plug into a PC, power the whole system, flash code, and go.

To understand why this is a catastrophic mistake on industrial hardware, look back at the original open hardware masterwork: the **Arduino UNO**.

```text
[ Arduino UNO (20 Years of Design Wisdom) ]
   ├── [ USB Type-B (Square) ] ──> Sketch Upload & Telemetry (5V / 500mA)
   └── [ 2.1mm DC Barrel Jack (Round) ] ──> Motor / Relay / Real Load Power (7-12V, 2A+)
```

For two decades, through dozens of revisions, Arduino stubbornly refused to eliminate the 2.1mm round DC barrel jack.

Why? Because PC USB ports are **fundamentally inadequate power sources** for real-world physical computing. A USB 2.0 port provides at most **5V @ 500mA (2.5W)**. The moment you stack a shield driving stepper motors, relays, or high-draw sensors, power requirements easily jump to **1A to 2A (5W to 10W)**.

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                       THE REAL-WORLD POWER GAP                         │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Standard PC USB 2.0 Port :  5V × 0.5A =  2.5W  (Bare MCU / Logic only) │
│ Real Actuator Load (Card):  5V × 2.0A = 10.0W  (4× deficit!)           │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The genius of the Arduino UNO was its **physical affordance**: the square USB port and the round DC jack felt completely different. You never confused code development with field deployment.

### The Modern Trap: Type-C Homogenization
Fast-forward to 2026: 5V 2A–3A USB Type-C wall adapters have replaced the round DC wall wart everywhere on Earth.

If an industrial board puts two identical Type-C ports side-by-side and allows the communication port to power the system, it creates **the most toxic user experience in embedded engineering**:

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   THE "HALF-WORKING" DEBUG HELL                        │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Developer plugs a single USB-C cable from their laptop to the board. │
│ 2. The power LED lights up.                                            │
│ 3. The microcontroller boots, enumerates, and accepts firmware.        │
│ 4. Serial telemetry streams across the screen perfectly.              │
│ 5. BUT: Expansion Cards (relays, motors, 4-20mA sensors) DO NOT MOVE.  │
│ 6. Developer spends 4 hours debugging initialization routines, I2C     │
│    pull-ups, and register maps...                                      │
│ 7. ...only to realize: "Oh. The external supply wasn't plugged in."    │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> **A system that half-works and masks its failure is worse than a system that is completely dead.**  
> If external power is missing, the board should not power up by a single millivolt. That way, the engineer knows in exactly one second what is wrong.

## 4. The CORE-CX5 Power Topology: Total Physical Separation

On CORE-CX5, we adopted the strict discipline of industrial PLCs: **maintenance ports do not supply field power.**

```text
[ 01: Comm Type-C (16P) ] ──> [ D+ / D- ESD ] ──> CH32X035 (PC16/PC17 USB PHY)
   (VBUS: Strictly N.C. / Unconnected)

[ 02: Power Type-C (6P) ] ──> [ TVS Diode ] ──> [ eFuse (SY6280) ] ──> VBUS_PROT (Bus Pins 17-20)
   (5.1k Rd, Dedicated In)                             │                       │
                                               [ POW_SW: EN Control ]   ├──> [ 3.3V LDO ] ──> MCU
                                                                        └──> Expansion Cards / Relays
```

1. **VBUS on the Comm Port is Completely N.C. (Not Connected)**:
   There is zero copper connecting the Comm Type-C VBUS pin to the board. The risk of back-feeding high voltage into a $2,000 laptop motherboard is physically **0%**.
2. **Arc-Free Power Switching via eFuse**:
   Rather than passing 2A through a mechanical slide switch (which causes contact degradation and arcing), the board's `POW_SW` pulls the active-high `EN` pin of an on-board **SY6280AAC eFuse** to ground. The switch handles a negligible **0.5 mA**, while the eFuse provides built-in soft-start and programmable over-current limiting (**1.5A to 2.0A**).
3. **Latching DFU Switch with Hardwired Warning LED**:
   Momentary boot buttons (holding a button while inserting a cable) require two hands. CORE-CX5 uses an **alternate-action latching switch (`HX PS5.8x5.8-6P`)**. Because it is DPDT (double-pole double-throw), pole 1 drives the MCU boot pin, while pole 2 **physically powers an ultra-bright red warning LED directly from 3.3V**. If the board is left in DFU mode, the red LED stays lit regardless of software state.
4. **Local Transient Decoupling (`06-C1`)**:
   Independent of the LDO's phase-compensation capacitor, an extra **100nF** ceramic capacitor (`06-C1`) is placed within **1.5 mm** of MCU Pin 2 (`VDD`) and the exposed thermal ground pad (`EPAD`) to suppress high-frequency transient switching sag during 48 MHz RISC-V execution.

## 5. Tearing Down Our Own Hype: From "Gravity Fail-Safe" to Honest Panel Topology

Engineering honesty also means correcting your own documentation when you catch yourself writing marketing fiction.

In our earlier drafts of the **ADX 20-Pin Bus Specification**, we justified placing the high-current `VBUS` cluster at the bottom row (Pins 17–20) with a dramatic story:
> *"If a screw terminal loosens, gravity causes the wire to drop downward, safely avoiding contact with communication lines above it ('Gravity Fail-Safe')."*

While it sounded clever, it was an after-the-fact narrative. Ribbon cables don't drop under gravity, and loose wires don't reliably fall in neat parabolas.

We rewrote the specification to align with real-world control cabinet axioms: **"Low-voltage signals on top, high-current power on the bottom."**

```text
  [ Cabinet Ducts ]        [ 8748 Module Front Edge ]              [ 20-Pin IDC Backplane ]
  ┌──────────────┐         ┌─────────────────────────┐            ┌────────────────────┐
  │ Upper Duct   │ ──────> │ Upper I/O: RS-485 / Com │ ─────────> │ Pins 7–15: Signals │ (Low-Voltage)
  │ (Sensors/Bus)│         └─────────────────────────┘            └────────────────────┘
  └──────────────┘                      │                                    │
                                 PCB Inner Artwork                    PCB Inner Artwork
                                (Upper Signal Traces)                (Upper Signal Traces)
                                        │                                    │
  ┌──────────────┐         ┌─────────────────────────┐            ┌────────────────────┐
  │ Lower Duct   │ ──────> │ Lower I/O: 5V High-Power│ ─────────> │ Pins 17–20: VBUS   │ (High-Current)
  │ (Main Power) │         └─────────────────────────┘            └────────────────────┘
  └──────────────┘
```

By placing Pins 17–20 at the bottom edge of the backplane connector:
- Heavy copper power planes flow in a **pure straight line** from the IDC connector to the front-edge high-current screw terminal.
- There are **zero cross-overs** on the PCB: power planes never cross under sensitive analog inputs (`Pin 3: AIN`) or high-speed SPI busses (`Pins 12–15`), eliminating switching ripple injection and IR drop interference by physical layout.

We formally released this as **[ADX-BUS-SPEC-v4.1](https://github.com/Masafuro/ADX/blob/main/docs/spec/ADX_PINOUT.md)** today.

Furthermore, we assigned the MCU's 2-wire serial debug lines directly to the IDC connector: **Pin 2 (`OWD: SWDIO`)** and **Pin 5 (`EXTCLK: SWCLK`)**. Because Pin 5 is guarded on both sides by Pin 4 and Pin 6 (`GND`), it forms a coplanar waveguide shield ideal for fast clock edges. In manufacturing, a single 20-pin ribbon cable powers the board, runs automated diagnostics, and flashes the bootloader—**zero pogo-pin test fixtures required**.
