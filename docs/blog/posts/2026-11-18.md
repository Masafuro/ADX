In our last log (*"The Battle of Millimeters"*), we zoomed in on the mechanical side of the **8854 Form Factor** (88.0 mm × 54.0 mm): dropping corner chamfers for clean V-cuts and shifting M3 mounting holes inward by 1 mm so washers don't chew up PCB edges.

Once the board outline settled down, we turned back to the harder question: **what actually belongs on the main MCU board?**

Over the past nine days, our USB core board (**`ADX CORE-U`**) went through four rapid architectural revisions (`rev1` through `rev4`). Each revision didn't add features—it deleted them. Here is the engineering trail behind why we stopped competing with general-purpose 32-bit dev boards and stripped `CORE-U` down to the bare essentials for 3D-printed enclosures.

---

## 1. The Challenge: Breadboard Heroes Struggle Inside Boxes

On paper, building a new board around an 8-bit **ATtiny1616** (16 KB Flash, 2 KB SRAM, 3.3V @ 10 MHz) looks ridiculous next to a $4 Raspberry Pi Pico or an Arduino UNO R4 Minima.

We stopped trying to win that spec sheet comparison. Instead, we surveyed real build logs from makers putting Arduino and ESP32 boards inside 3D-printed enclosures. The failure modes are rarely "not enough CPU MHz." They are almost always physical and electrical:

- **Brownouts on 500 mA PC USB**: Everything works on the desk until you drive a motor, relay, or LED strip inside the case.
- **Trapped Reset / BOOT Buttons**: Once the lid is screwed shut, a firmware hang that drops the MCU's native USB stack forces you to crack open the enclosure.
- **Mounting & Wiring Friction**: Boards without consistent M3 mounting holes end up hot-glued in place, while loose Dupont jumper wires vibrate free or snag against enclosure walls.

So we drew a clean line in our lineup:

- **`PRO` Grade** (`CORE-A24 PRO`): USB-less, vibration-tested spring terminals, isolated RS-485, full industrial BOM temperature qualification.
- **Standard Grade** (`CORE-U`): A tough, low-cost USB core designed specifically to survive inside 3D-printed enclosures and desktop rigs.

We also archived our Grove-focused `CORE-G` concept. Non-locking `HY2.0` / `XH` friction headers and generic third-party cables made it nearly impossible to define consistent electrical or mechanical test boundaries.

## 2. Four Revisions of Subtraction (rev1 → rev4)

Looking back at `rev1` through `rev4`, the pattern is clear—every time we tried to make the core board "helpful" by adding onboard peripherals, we made expansion and assembly worse:

| Revision | Key Additions / Removals | JLCPCB PCBA (120 pcs)* | Why It Changed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`rev1`** | Grove (`HY2.0`) ×2, `XH-4AW`, 6-pin USB-C, piezo buzzer | — | Ambiguous non-locking connectors blocked consistent testability; 6-pin USB-C lacked mechanical THT anchor specs. |
| **`rev2`** | Added microSD, panel switch/LED spring terminals, `TPS2116` ORing mux | `$572.81` (`$4.77`/pc) | Realized stuffing peripherals onto the core board repeats the *Maker UNO* trap—leaving less room and power headroom for real expansion. |
| **`rev3`** | Dropped microSD & ORing IC; went all-SMD; physically split `USB-PWR` / `USB-COM` | `$465.74` (`$3.88`/pc) | Much cleaner, but lingering PC-style buzzer/pin headers still created a second expansion path and extra QA test points. |
| **`rev4`** | **Stripped all extra headers & buzzer.** Unified on **one 20P SMD IDC (`CN1`)** + 4 LEDs | **`$460.57` (`$3.83`/pc)** | Zero ambiguity: one expansion bus, one power topology, full SMD assembly. |

*\*Rough estimates including JLCPCB PCBA and shipping at 120-unit batch scale (targeting ~$15.35 at 4× BOM).*

Remember our earlier post on `CORE-CX5` (*"Why We Killed 'USB One-Cable Debug' on Our RISC-V Industrial Controller"*), where a 2-chip ATtiny1616 + USB bridge looked painfully expensive in small batches? Moving **100% SMD**—including switching the 20-pin 2.54 mm IDC box header (`CN1`: `321020MG0CBK00A01`) and 16-pin Type-C receptacles (`UC09-0B16F00P11`) to surface-mount—eliminated wave/hand-soldering steps and pulled the 120-piece unit cost down to **`$3.83`**.

> **Key Takeaway:**  
> A microcontroller core inside an enclosure shouldn't hoard buzzers, SD slots, or miscellaneous pin headers. Its job is to provide clean power protection, unbrickable programming, and one standardized bus—pushing everything else onto modular expansion Cards.

## 3. Three Engineering Choices Inside `CORE-U rev4`

```text
                      [ ADX CORE-U rev4 Architecture ]

   [ 5V/3A Wall Adapter ] ──▶ USB-PWR (Type-C) ──▶ [ SMF5.0CA TVS ]
                                                          │
                                                          ▼
                                             [ AP22814AW5-7 eFuse ] ──▶ VBUS_PROT (5V / 2.0A)
                                               ├── FLG ──▶ PA7/FLG        ├──▶ CN1 Pins 17–20
                                               └── EN (10kΩ PU)           ├──▶ POW_LED (Green)
                                                                          └──▶ [ ME6211 3.3V LDO ]
                                                                                    │
   [ PC (Arduino IDE 1.8) ] ─▶ USB-COM (Type-C) ─▶ [ USBLC6 ESD ]                  ▼
     (VBUS Physically N.C.)                         │                       MCU VDD & CH342K
                                                    ▼
                                            [ WCH CH342K (3.3V) ]
                                              ├── Port 0 ──▶ PA0/UPDI (SerialUPDI + CN1 Pin 2)
                                              └── Port 1 ──▶ PA1/TX, PA2/RX (Alt-UART Debug)
```

### 1. Physically Split Type-C Ports (Zero ORing IC)
Instead of paying for a power-multiplexer IC like the `TPS2116` to arbitrate between a PC and a wall adapter, `rev4` separates the two roles at the copper level:

- **`USB-PWR`** takes 5V (up to 2.0A / 10W) through a `SMF5.0CA-MS` TVS diode and an **`AP22814AW5-7`** high-side load switch / eFuse (3A continuous max, 50 mΩ `RDS(on)`, over-current/short/thermal/reverse-current protection). Its active-low `FLG` fault pin feeds straight into `PA7/FLG` so the MCU can react—even autonomously via ATtiny1616's CCL hardware logic—if an expansion load trips.
- **`USB-COM`** leaves `VBUS` **completely disconnected** (with 5.1 kΩ `Rd` resistors on `CC1`/`CC2` for host detection and `USBLC6-2SC6` on `D+`/`D-`). And because the `CH342K` is powered from the board's `3V3` LDO (`ME6211C33M5G-N`), there is zero back-powering into the MCU when external power is off.

### 2. Flash Without Closing Your Serial Monitor—or Opening the Box
By pairing an external **`WCH CH342K`** (Dual USB-UART) with the ATtiny1616's native **`UPDI`** on `PA0`:

- **Port 0** runs **`SerialUPDI`** for programming. Because `PA0` stays configured as UPDI (no fuse flip to `RESET`, no bootloader needed, no 12V HV-UPDI recovery required), you can reflash a hung chip inside a sealed 3D-printed box without touching a reset button.
- **Port 1** connects to `PA1/TX` and `PA2/RX` (`Serial.swap(1)` in `megaTinyCore`). That leaves `PB2/TX` and `PB3/RX` (`Serial.swap(0)`) free on `CN1` Pins 7–8 for expansion Cards, while letting you keep the Arduino IDE 1.8.x Serial Monitor open permanently during upload cycles.

### 3. Four LEDs Onboard, Everything Else Over `CN1`
In `rev4`, even the SMD piezo buzzer didn't survive the cut. The board keeps only a green `POW_LED` plus three GPIO-driven status LEDs (`LED_R`, `LED_Y`, `LED_W`) on spare pins—enough to blink error states or heartbeat before sealing a box, while leaving audible alarms and actuators to the 20-pin IDC bus.

---

## 4. Honest Trade-offs & Where Things Stand

Keeping the core board this minimal means accepting two clear trade-offs:

1. **Two cables on the bench**: Because `USB-COM` cannot power the board, you *must* plug in `USB-PWR` even for quick desk tests.
2. **Not a standalone breakout**: Without discrete screw terminals or pin headers on the PCB, hooking up external hardware requires plugging an ADX expansion Card (or ribbon breakout) into `CN1`.

Right now, the `rev4` netlist is in final cleanup—wiring up the remaining `CH342K`, MCU power pins, and LED current-limiting resistors before we start PCB routing.
